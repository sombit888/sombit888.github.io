TL;DR: We teach a 3B vision-language model to locate objects in 3D from a single image, using non-robotic images with 3D annotations that are cheap to make. We then train a robot policy on top of it. The resulting model, SPEAR-1, matches or beats π0-FAST and π0.5 while using 20× fewer robot demonstrations. The weights are open.

Abstract

Robotic Foundation Models (RFMs) hold great promise as generalist, end-to-end systems for robot control. Yet their ability to generalize across new environments, tasks, and embodiments remains limited. We argue that a major bottleneck lies in their foundations: most RFMs are built by fine-tuning internet-pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs). However, these VLMs are trained on 2D image-language tasks and lack the 3D spatial reasoning inherently required for embodied control in the 3D world. Bridging this gap directly with large-scale robotic data is costly and difficult to scale. Instead, we propose to enrich easy-to-collect non-robotic image data with 3D annotations and enhance a pretrained VLM with 3D understanding capabilities. Following this strategy, we train SPEAR-VLM, a 3D-aware VLM that infers object coordinates in 3D space from a single 2D image. Building on SPEAR-VLM, we introduce our main contribution, SPEAR-1: a robotic foundation model that integrates grounded 3D perception with language-instructed embodied control. Trained on ∼45M frames from 24 Open X-Embodiment datasets, SPEAR-1 outperforms or matches state-of-the-art models such as π0-FAST and π0.5, while it uses 20× fewer robot demonstrations. This carefully-engineered training strategy unlocks new VLM capabilities and as a consequence boosts the reliability of embodied control beyond what is achievable with only robotic data. We make our model weights and 3D-annotated datasets publicly available.

A flat view of a 3D world

A robotic foundation model takes camera images and an instruction in plain language, and outputs the motor commands that complete the task. Unlike today's specialized robots, it should work in new environments and on different robots without being programmed again for every task.

Most of these models are vision-language-action models (VLAs). They are built from three parts:

  • a large language model, which brings reasoning and knowledge about the world;
  • a vision module that understands 2D images and, together with the language model, forms a vision-language model (VLM);
  • a control module that turns the VLM's understanding into 3D positions for the robot to move to.

VLAs start from a pretrained VLM. These VLMs recognize and describe scenes very well, but they are essentially 2D. They do not know how far apart two objects are or how big they are. A robot needs exactly this 3D geometry to act. In practice, VLAs can look impressive in short videos and then fail as soon as object positions, layouts or backgrounds change. SPEAR-1 is built to close this gap.

How SPEAR-1 works

We train SPEAR-1 in two stages. First, we extend a VLM from flat 2D pixels to the 3D world using openly available, non-robotic data. This gives SPEAR-VLM. Then we attach a control module and train the whole model to imitate robot movements recorded with human teleoperation. Each stage uses data that is more expensive to collect than the one before.

Stage 1: teaching a VLM about 3D (SPEAR-VLM)

VLMs such as GPT-5, Gemini or Claude are pretrained on web data, with tasks like answering questions about an image or finding an object in it. That knowledge stays in the 2D image plane. We start from a smaller, 3-billion-parameter VLM and add a depth model that estimates how far every pixel is from the camera. We then train it to answer geometric questions such as “Provide the 3D bounding box coordinates of the bowl” or “Estimate the x, y, z distance between the mug and the spoon”. To answer, SPEAR-VLM has to find the object from its description, estimate its 3D coordinates and reason about distances between objects.

Stage 2: from 3D understanding to actions (SPEAR-1)

SPEAR-VLM outputs language. To control a robot, we attach a control module that outputs motor commands and 3D target positions in real time. We train it with flow matching, a variant of diffusion models, to imitate trajectories that people recorded by teleoperating robots through each task many times. Because the backbone already understands 3D, SPEAR-1 needs about 20× fewer robot demonstrations than comparable models.

Results

All evaluations are zero-shot: we fine-tune neither SPEAR-1 nor the baselines for the test tasks or environments. On a Franka arm in the DROID setup we compare with Physical Intelligence's π0-FAST and π0.5. Both are trained on at least 900M more robot frames, collected in more diverse environments, than SPEAR-1's ~45M, but neither uses 3D pretraining. π0-FAST and π0.5 are not available for WidowX, so on that robot we compare with OpenVLA, a model about twice SPEAR-1's size, and SpatialVLA.

On Franka, SPEAR-1 reaches about 5× the task progress of π0-FAST, which shares its π0-style architecture. It also matches π0.5, which adds architectural improvements and high-level subtask prediction and is trained in at least 5× more environments. On WidowX, SPEAR-1 outperforms OpenVLA, which shows that it carries over to a different robot. Together, these results suggest that 3D knowledge about the world can substitute for a large share of robot data.

Limitations and next steps

  • The 3D pretraining is not well suited to deformable objects or objects with complex shapes. Other 3D priors, such as rotation estimation or collision detection, could capture their geometry better.
  • The 3D box labels come from MoGe's affine-invariant depth, so they are not in metric space. Ground-truth point clouds or metric depth estimators could fix this.
  • We do not yet know how robot performance scales with the amount and quality of 3D pretraining data.
  • SPEAR-1 still needs fine-tuning on a new robot embodiment to work well. It remains to be seen how it compares to models such as π0.5 across many more tasks and environments.

More broadly, robotic foundation models still have a long way to go: long-horizon planning, autonomous self-improvement and completing unseen tasks. We believe other cheap sources of data can bring gains similar to those 3D data brought here. If you are interested in collaborating, please reach out.
