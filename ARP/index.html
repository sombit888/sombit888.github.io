TL;DR: We run the large vision-language model and the small action network of a robot policy as two separate processes. The robot reacts to every new camera frame without waiting for the large model, and the large model only needs to update now and then.

Abstract

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) control robots by running a vision-language model (VLM) and an action expert one after the other for every chunk of actions. The time from observation to action therefore grows with the size of the VLM, which matters whenever the scene changes while the robot is moving. We propose Asynchronous Robot Policies (ARP), which run the two parts as independent processes at different rates. The VLM periodically writes a fixed-size semantic memory. A lightweight vision-action expert with its own camera input reads the most recent memory and produces actions at a high rate. Because that memory is always somewhat out of date, we train the expert on memories of varying age and condition it on that age and on its own execution delay. In simulation, when the target object is moved during the reach, ARP-1B succeeds in 51.5% of episodes against 9.5% for a π0-style baseline with the same backbone. On LIBERO it keeps 79.0% success with the VLM refreshed only once every 4 seconds, where the baseline drops to 45.3%. On a Franka robot, a DROID-trained ARP-3B reaches 78, 73 and 80% task progress on three tasks, against 32, 80 and 50% for π0.5-DROID. On the broader RoboLab benchmark it remains well behind π0.5-DROID (20.0 vs 37.9%).

Big models, slow reflexes

Most general-purpose robot policies today are built on vision-language models, the kind of neural network that can describe an image or answer questions about it. A vision-language-action model (VLA), such as π0 or π0.5, adds an action expert: a smaller network that turns the VLM's understanding of the scene and the instruction into motor commands. At every step the robot takes a picture, runs the VLM, runs the action expert, and executes a short chunk of motions before repeating.

This loop works when nothing moves. A plan made half a second ago is still a good plan if the cup is where it was. It breaks down when the world changes during the motion, for example when an object gets nudged or a ball rolls. The robot keeps executing a plan based on an old picture, and the next plan has to wait for the VLM again. A larger VLM understands more, and it also makes this wait longer.

Two processes, two speeds

ARP splits the policy into two processes that never wait for each other. The large VLM, which we call the semantic expert, looks at the camera image and the instruction from time to time and writes what it has understood into a memory of fixed size. A much smaller vision-action expert runs continuously. Each time it is called, it looks at the current camera images, reads whichever memory is newest, and outputs the next actions.

The fast loop never runs the VLM, so the robot's reaction time no longer depends on how big the VLM is. On one RTX A6000 GPU, a VLM call takes 64 ms at 1B parameters and 120 ms at 8B, while the action expert takes about 61 ms at every size:

The catch: the memory is always out of date

Running the two processes independently has a price. The memory the action expert reads was computed from an older image, sometimes a fifth of a second old and sometimes several seconds. A model that only saw fresh memories during training does not cope with that.

We deal with this in training. Each training example pairs the current camera frames of a demonstration with a memory computed from an earlier frame of the same demonstration, up to 2 s earlier, and the action expert is told how old the memory is. Architecture

Semantic expert. In simulation this is InternVL3.5 at 1B to 14B parameters, and on DROID it is PaliGemma-3B. It reads one image and the instruction. We take the keys and values of 8 of its layers. A LayerNorm and a linear projection map them to a memory M with a fixed number of 768-wide tokens. We then add a position embedding and an embedding of the memory's age. M has the same shape for every VLM, so nothing downstream changes when the VLM does.

Vision-action expert. A 12-block transformer (183M parameters in simulation). It encodes the current external and wrist images and cross-attends to M in every block. It predicts a 20-step action chunk by flow matching with 5 Euler steps. Adaptive RMS normalisation conditions every block on the flow time, the memory age d and the expert's own execution delay ℓ.

Training. The memory age is sampled as d ~ 0.2·δ0 + 0.8·U{1, …, 40} control steps. The expert also learns to continue a given prefix of actions, as in real-time chunking, so consecutive chunks join smoothly.

On a real robot

We compared ARP with π0.5-DROID, a strong, openly available VLA from Physical Intelligence. The robot is a Franka FR3 arm in the DROID setup, with one camera facing the table and one on the wrist, and inference runs on one RTX A6000. Our model, ARP-3B, uses PaliGemma-3B as the large model and a vision-action expert built on DINOv3 image features. It was trained only on the public DROID dataset. π0.5-DROID was, in addition, pre-trained by its authors on a larger robot dataset that is not public. ARP ran asynchronously, and the action expert continued from the last 8 actions it had committed to.

Each policy attempted each of three tasks 10 times, with a limit of 900 control steps, about 90 s. We score the final state: the fraction of objects in the bowl, or of cups nested.

When the scene changes

Benchmarks like LIBERO hardly measure reaction time, because nothing moves unless the robot moves it. We built two simulated tests in which the scene changes while the robot is acting.

  • In LIBERO-Perturb, the object the robot is reaching for jumps 5 cm sideways when the gripper gets within 10 cm of it. It runs on 20 LIBERO tasks.
  • In Dyn-Bump, a ball or cylinder rolls toward the robot and is knocked onto a new course, turned by up to 100°, at a random moment 1.5 to 5 s into the episode. The robot has to catch it and lift it.

Both tests are deterministic, so every policy faces exactly the same episodes.

We compare three versions of the same 1B model:

  • ARP runs asynchronously.
  • ARP weights, synchronous uses the same trained network but calls the VLM before every action chunk, as an ordinary VLA would.
  • The π0-style baseline is our model with the action expert's camera input removed, so it sees the scene only through the VLM's memory, as π0 does. It shares our backbone, data and training recipe.

The two tests separate two effects. When the object jumps mid-reach, all three policies first reach toward where it used to be, in about 76% of episodes, because the reach is already under way. ARP then recovers in 46% of those episodes and the π0-style baseline in 7%. The synchronous version of ARP does almost as well as the asynchronous one (46.5 vs 51.5%), so in this test the gain comes from giving the action expert its own camera rather than from running asynchronously.

When the object keeps moving, asynchrony matters too. The same weights drop from 44.7% to 31.3% when the VLM is put back into the control loop. With a bump in only half of the episodes, the results are 48.0% and 36.4% over 450 episodes each, and 20.0% for the π0-style baseline over 150.

We also measured how long the arm takes to turn toward the ball's new course after a bump. Asynchronous execution turned sooner on average in all five comparisons we ran, by 135 to 261 ms. None of these differences is statistically significant at our sample sizes.

Running the large model less often

Since the action expert does the reacting, the VLM does not have to keep pace with the robot. We kept the action expert at 5 Hz and refreshed the memory less and less often, on LIBERO, with 400 episodes per point.

A broad benchmark: RoboLab

Does a bigger VLM help?

In simulation we trained the same recipe with five sizes of InternVL3.5, from 1B to 14B parameters. The vision encoder is the same frozen InternViT-300M in every model, so only the language model changes. On the standard LIBERO benchmark (40 tasks, 2,000 episodes per model), success rises from 84.8% at 1B to 89.3% at 8B. 14B is no better than 8B. When the object jumps mid-reach, larger models did not help: 51.5% at 1B, 40.0% at 8B and 40.0% at 14B.

Limitations

The real-robot study is small: three tasks, ten trials each, one robot and one lab. The two policies were run in separate sessions on the same day, and π0.5-DROID did better on cup stacking.

ARP-3B is not a better generalist than π0.5-DROID. It trails on RoboLab by 17.9 points overall. The two models differ in both architecture and training data, so this comparison cannot tell which of the two matters more.

We have not shown that a larger VLM makes the robot react better. On LIBERO-Perturb the larger models did worse. In that test, most of ARP's advantage comes from the action expert's own camera, and only a little from asynchrony. Before running it, we registered a check that asynchronous execution stalls less often than synchronous execution there, and the check failed: 12.0% against 12.5% of episodes stalled for more than a second. Reaction times favour asynchronous execution but are not significant, and settling the question would take roughly ten times more trials.

We do not know how slowly the VLM can run before performance drops. We tested down to one refresh every 4 s. In simulation, the π0-style baseline is our reimplementation, not the released π0. The real-robot and RoboLab comparisons use the released π0.5-DROID.
